The funnel is the story

Retrieval reaches 10.7% of the catalog. By the time the list is cut to what a listener sees, 3.0% remains. Most of the catalog is not being ranked badly — it is never a candidate at all.

Distinct tracks appearing at least once across all 400 queries. The catalog is already the filtered one: Cadence discards tracks on fewer than four playlists before any of this runs.

One surprise, in Cadence's favour

58.5% of what Cadence shows comes from the long tail — the bottom half of the catalog by playlist count — against 54% of the catalog being tail. A tail lift of 1.09×. The system is not popularity-biased in the usual sense. Its concentration problem is about artists, not about hits.

Any one playlist looks fine

A median of 17 distinct artists in the 20 tracks shown per query, and only 5 of 400 queries return a single artist. Per-playlist diversity is not the problem.

The concentration is across queries: the same artists reappear for different requests. A diversity metric computed per playlist — which is the usual way this gets measured — cannot see that, which is why the audit is run over the whole battery and reported as one distribution.

What it costs to intervene

Two knobs, swept over the same cached candidates so the only thing differing between conditions is the intervention. A popularity penalty asks is this track popular?; an artist cap asks has this artist been heard already?

Popularity penalty Artist cap

Up and to the right is better: more long-tail exposure at less accuracy cost. The circled point is the system as it ships.

The best cheap trade on offer: a penalty of 0.2 moves long-tail share 58.5% → 62.9% (+4.5 points) for -9.0% R-precision. Whether that is worth paying is a product decision, which is the point of measuring it instead of guessing.

Seeing it on one query

Two cases, deliberately paired. The first is a theme, where concentration is a defect the penalty can fix. The second is an artist's name, where concentration is the correct answer and the penalty makes the result worse without improving artist diversity at all — it just digs for more obscure tracks by the same artist.

The finding that matters

Across every intervention tested — nine penalty strengths and four artist caps — artist Gini moves only from 0.951 to 0.936. You cannot re-rank your way out of a retrieval problem.

Concentration is not being created by the ranking function. It is already present in the candidate pool, and re-ordering a hundred candidates cannot introduce the 89% of the catalog that never became a candidate. The popularity penalty does move long-tail share substantially — it works on the metric it targets — and barely touches artist equality, because a few artists owning many catalog entries is not a fact about any single track's popularity.

That lines up with Timbre's result from the other direction: 76.6% of distinct tracks are filtered out before Cadence's index is even built. The exposure ceiling is set upstream of everything measured here.

Honest limits

  • This audits the retrieval and ranking stage. Cadence's final assembly applies MMR and a two-per-artist cap of its own, so the shipped playlist is more diverse than the top-20 measured here. The funnel numbers are about candidate reach, which is the binding constraint either way.
  • Exposure is counted over 400 queries. Coverage grows with query volume — the concentration ratios are the stable quantities, the absolute coverage percentages are not.
  • Popularity is proxied by playlist count in the MPD sample. It is a reasonable stand-in for exposure, not a measurement of streams or revenue.
  • The interventions are deliberately simple. A calibrated or provider-fair re-ranker would likely find better points on this frontier; the contribution here is the frontier itself, and a measurement harness to place any future method on it.
