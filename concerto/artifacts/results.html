What actually stops a scalper

4,232 seats, $95 face, 8x oversubscribed. Eight allocation policies, each solved to a rational-expectations equilibrium against a broker sector that decides for itself how many identities to buy.

No policy makes a hot ticket cheap. Across all 180 parameter cells, every arm leaves the average fan paying more than 1.2x face. What a policy chooses is not the price — it is who gets in, and who keeps the difference.

Where a fan's money goes

Under the queue that ships today, a fan pays $221 per ticket and $19 of it reaches a broker. Under the strictest arm they pay $121 and none of it does. Under market clearing they pay $291 — more than the queue — and the difference goes to the artist instead.

Every arm can be placed on two axes: what a fan pays, and whether the people who have listened for years actually got in. Market clearing eliminates the broker by becoming one. Affinity rationing is the only thing in the set that moves the vertical axis at all.

Three knobs, priced

The policy document recommended holding 20% of the house back for an open draw, selling 15% at a clearing price, and setting the purchase cap at party size — before any of the three had been measured. One turned out right for a reason that was not the reason given, one turned out to have no optimum at all, and one turned out to be conditional on something the assertion never mentioned.

Holding seats back for an open draw

Affinity rationing serves 96% of superfan demand and 0.0% of casual demand — a merit cut is absolute, so a fan one place below it has no chance at all rather than a small one. Superfan access is completely flat until 20% is reserved, because top-decile demand does not fill the house: the reserve is free until it eats into the slack, and roughly one-for-one after that.

At 20% reserved, casual fans get 2.8% of their demand against 12.5% under a pure lottery — about a fifth of the way back, not a restoration.

How tight to set the purchase cap

Tightening the cap from 8 to 2 truncates 20.1% of family ticket demand either way — the same families, the same parties split. What it buys depends entirely on whether the resale margin is still open, which is why this sweep runs against two arms. A single-arm version would have produced a confident number and a coin-flip as to which of two contradictory conclusions got published.

This curve has no knee. Revenue gained per point of low-income access lost falls monotonically from the very first seat, so the sweep does not identify an optimum — it prices a choice. Any share picked here is a judgement about what artist revenue is worth, and has to be defended as one rather than as a discovered optimum.

What the chain can enforce

A Cardano validator can refuse any transfer, cap any resale price and take a royalty on every hop. It cannot see a broker sell the wallet. A smart contract can escrow an asset; it cannot escrow a secret, because a secret that has been shown has been given away.

A validator-capped resale and a fully soulbound token are entirely different contracts and produce the same broker economics — 7% and 7% of the original spread. The only rung that closes it is the one that is not on the chain at all: an identity check at the door.

And the drop itself does not fit on chain

In the eUTxO model a transaction consumes specific outputs, so a single inventory UTxO sells one seat per block. Sharding the house across many UTxOs fixes the throughput and converts the problem into a retry storm: at 100 shards against 40,000 buyers, 0.25% of submitted transactions succeed — about 400 signed, submitted, rejected transactions per person who gets a ticket.

The one fitted parameter

The model has a single free constant: what a usable purchasing identity costs a broker. It is fitted by bisection so the unrestricted arm reproduces a 3x resale markup — the only quantity here that is publicly observable. Broker capture is an output, not a target.
